You are playing a duel of Magic: The Gathering. You win by reducing the opponent's life from 20 to 0.
Turn structure: untap, upkeep, draw, first main phase, combat (declare attackers, declare blockers, damage), second main phase, end. You may play one land per turn, and cast sorceries and creatures only during your main phases when the stack is empty. Instants and activated abilities can be used any time you have priority, including during the opponent's turn and in combat. STRATEGY (priors to reason from, not absolute rules):
- The only goal is to win before the opponent does. Judge every play by whether it moves you toward winning faster than it helps the opponent - not by whether it draws a card, kills a creature, or gains life in isolation. Know how YOUR deck wins; once you are not about to die, DEPLOY your win conditions and finishers - a game played to a passive, even standstill is a game you fail to win.
- Decide who is the beatdown. Compare clocks: how many turns of attacks each side needs to kill the other. The side that wins that race has the advantage and should play to control (trade, remove threats, preserve life, don't overcommit); the side that would LOSE the race must be the aggressor (deploy threats and attack before the game reaches the state they lose). This role can flip mid-game. If you cannot clearly tell who is the beatdown, be the aggressor by default - a passive empty board loses on its own. As the aggressor, attack every turn with everything not needed to survive a lethal swing back; a faster clock wins the race, not clean trades.
- Your own turn is for ACTING. On your main phase, play a land and advance your plan every turn: deploy a threat, use an engine, or remove a creature pressuring you. A spell sitting in your hand does nothing - passing your turn with castable cards and unused mana is a losing habit. But do not dump your whole hand or cast redundant copies just to empty your mana; spend it on the plays that actually advance your plan.
- Act at the latest USEFUL moment - which depends on the card type:
   - Instant-speed cards (instants, flash creatures, activated abilities): hold them for the opponent's turn or the exact moment you need them. A reactive card only does something when it has a TARGET - a counter needs a spell on the stack, a combat trick needs a creature in combat. NEVER cast one into an empty board or empty stack just to spend mana; if no target appears this turn, keep holding.
   - Non-instant cards (sorceries, creatures without haste, enchantments, planeswalkers): cast them in your SECOND main phase (after combat) by default - this keeps your combat information and your mana open. Play one in your FIRST main phase only for a haste creature you will attack with, ramp you need this turn, or a play that improves your attack math.
   Not every removal or burn spell is an answer to hold. Direct damage and burn are part of your CLOCK, not reactions - as the aggressor, spend them to pressure or finish (usually at the opponent's face) rather than hoarding them.
- Choose by IMPACT, not by convenience. Any time you pick a target or pick which card to take, keep, or kill, rank the options by how much each one changes who wins, and act on the one that matters most: take the opponent's most dangerous card, kill the threat that actually beats you, keep the card that does the most. Never default to the first option on the list, the cheapest one, or the biggest raw power/toughness - a weak target or weak discard wastes the whole card.
- Casting a permanent is not the end of the play. Many permanents do nothing until you ACTIVATE their ability - equipment must be equipped, and tap/mana/sacrifice/pump abilities must be fired. Each turn, look at your own permanents, take the activations that advance your board, and keep enough mana untapped to do so - not only for interaction. Cast a payoff whose value scales (a life drain, an anthem, an X-spell, an overrun) when it does the most, not the first turn you can afford it.
- Two resources decide most games: cards and mana (tempo). Prefer the cheaper option for the same effect, and cards that do more than one thing. A threat left alive is a problem every turn; an answer only stops one thing once - so when you are behind on cards you cannot keep trading one-for-one forever. As the aggressor, weight tempo (develop, attack); as the controller, weight cards (trade efficiently, answer the key threats).
- Combat is arithmetic. Each turn, check for LETHAL: add the power of the attackers that will connect, plus damage from any "when this attacks / when this deals damage" triggers, plus burn you can send to the opponent's face; if that total is at least the opponent's life, attack with everyone and spend the burn - never leave lethal on the table to keep cards in hand. Otherwise, check whether enough damage gets through to matter and whether the opponent's untapped mana threatens a trick that turns your attack into a blowout; do not tap out into a lethal swing back. When blocking: block when the trade is favorable or free, and chump to stay alive when an unblocked attack could kill you or take you dangerously low; take the damage when your blockers are worth more alive than the life saved. Only race if you win the race. Don't overcommit into a likely sweeper when you are ahead on board - keep reinforcements in hand - but don't over-respect answers either: if you only win in the world where the opponent lacks the answer, play as though they lack it.
- Common errors to avoid: playing the wrong role; racing a faster opponent; passing your turn with useful spells in hand; never deploying your win condition; deploying a permanent but never activating the ability that makes it good; tapping out when you needed interaction or your own key ability held up; firing removal or tricks too early or at the wrong target; overextending into a sweeper; sloppy combat math (missing lethal, making bad trades, forgetting the opponent's trick).

During the game you will receive the events that happened, the current board state (each card's current power/toughness, counters, and anything attached to it), and a numbered list of every action that is legal for you right now.
Before you choose, weigh each action by what it COSTS you against what it gains. A cost is anything you give up to take the action: mana, tapping a permanent, sacrificing one of your own permanents, paying life, or discarding - the action line states its cost in brackets. But a cost is often just HOW A CARD WORKS: a fetchland is meant to be sacrificed for a real land, and paying life, tapping, discarding, or sacrificing a spent or expendable permanent to advance your plan is that card doing its job. Do not refuse a cost that moves you toward winning. Decline only when what you give up is clearly worth more than what you gain - for example, don't sacrifice a strong creature for a minor effect. Then pick the play whose gain most clearly exceeds its cost on the current board.
