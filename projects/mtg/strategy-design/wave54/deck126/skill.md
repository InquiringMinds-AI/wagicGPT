## S5 - WHEN A RULE NAMES THE EXACT STRING IT IS BROKEN ON, THE MISSING PIECE IS NOT A BETTER
## STATEMENT OF THE RULE - IT IS WHAT THE STRING MEANS.

deck126's Rule #7 entry 1 already read: take the missing enchantment "at any `leaves N`, including
`casting this taps you out`". The pilot broke it twice, and **both breaks are on that clause**
(`126v146` seq 14 at 25 life, `126v162` seq 11 at 9 life). The rule had told it the clause was not
a stop and it still read the clause as a stop.

Rule: a clause the pilot keeps obeying against your instruction is a clause the pilot has a THEORY
about, and the guide has to answer the theory, not repeat the instruction. Here the theory is
"tapping out is dangerous", which is true in most decks and false in this one - deck126 holds one
instant, has no counterspell, and blocks with defenders whether its lands are tapped or not. The
edit says THAT, then re-states the instruction. Companion to #174's "the thought X is ALWAYS
FALSE" device and to #197: when a tag answers a question the pilot did not ask, tell it what the
tag is actually about.

## S6 - A TAG THAT COSTS THE PILOT LIFE BELONGS TO THE SEAT WHOSE LIFE IT COSTS, AND IT RENDERS ON
## THE OTHER PLAYER'S LINE.

Silverquill Silencer (`borderline.txt:102764`) prints `[named: <card>]` on the OPPONENT battlefield
line, and casting that card costs the seat 3 life and gives the opponent a card. The tag rendered
**54 times** across my two seats and the render is complete and correct - but every guide rule
about "read your battlefield line" and "read the row you are answering" points somewhere else, and
the cast row does not price it. deck126 cast the named card **2 of 2** times it cast it at all, for
6 life, in a game it lost by 5 (`126v146` seq 42 and seq 61).

Rule: extends #193. When you enumerate the dedicated lines a guide must read, **the opponent's
battlefield line is one of them whenever a tag there changes the PRICE of one of your own rows.**
The review question that finds these: for each new render, ask whose life it moves, then check
whether that seat's guide reads the line it is printed on. A tag on their board that taxes your
hand is invisible to a guide organised around your board and your row.

Sizing note (#198): the same tag sat on the line as `[named: Chromatic Lantern]` for 19 records
and `[named: Essence Scatter]` for 12 on deck125 with **zero** casts of either, so the failure is
one bullet's worth, not a section's - the pilot only walks into it when the named card is the card
the deck's whole plan is about.

## S7 - A GUIDE THAT PASSES EVERY PREDICTION IS EVIDENCE ABOUT THE PREDICTIONS, AND THE RIGHT
## OUTPUT IS THREE EDITS, NOT THIRTY.

deck126 passed D92 through D98 - the zero-Tribute stop went 2/39 to **0/99**, the dead-tutor stop
2/22 to **0/3**, the second-copy stop 1/3 to **0/24**, the reveal picks 7/9 to **10/10**, blockers
0 `BLOCKS: none` of 15, land drop 46/46 - and won 4 of 6 with the two losses carrying one new
finding each. Three edits were written. Two of them are new-shape items; the third is a
one-sentence refresh recording a ceiling.

Rule: **a wave in which a guide's predictions all pass is the wave to write the fewest lines**, and
the seat file's job that wave is to say which ceilings are now at their third corpus and therefore
freeze candidates. Growth in a passing guide is uncompensated risk: every added sentence is a new
surface for the #202/#178 over-aggression failure. Companion to #200(c) - a passing rule's
supporting anecdote is the cheapest text in the file to compress, and compressing it is how a
guide stays inside the band while gaining the one bullet it actually needed.
